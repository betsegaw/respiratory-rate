How the Breathing Rate Monitor Works

A from-scratch guide to the signal processing behind the monitor — no background needed. We'll build up every idea with plain words, everyday analogies, and little interactive toys you can play with, until you can see exactly how the app turns your phone's wobble into a breaths-per-minute number.

1What is a signal?

A signal is just a measurement that changes over time. If you wrote down a number every fraction of a second, a signal is the list of those numbers — or, more usefully, the shape they trace when you plot them with time running left to right.

Analogy. Think of a cork bobbing on the sea. Its height goes up and down as waves pass. If you filmed the cork and plotted its height over time, that wavy line is a signal. Your voice is a signal (air pressure over time). A heartbeat on a hospital monitor is a signal.

Our signal. When the phone lies on the child's chest, each breath gently tilts it. The phone's accelerometer always feels gravity pulling "down," and as the phone tilts, that downward pull gets shared differently across its three axes. So as he breathes, the gravity reading on each axis rises and falls a tiny bit. That rising-and-falling is the signal we care about. Everything below is about pulling a clean "breaths per minute" out of that faint wobble.

2Frequency, period, and hertz

A wave that repeats has two numbers that describe its rhythm:

  • Period — how long one full cycle takes (one up-and-down), measured in seconds.
  • Frequency — how many full cycles happen per second. Its unit is the hertz (Hz). 1 Hz means "one cycle every second."

They're two sides of the same coin: frequency = 1 ÷ period. A slow wave (long period) has a low frequency; a fast wave (short period) has a high frequency.

Breathing is slow, so its frequency is a small number. One breath every 2 seconds is 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 Hz. Because people say "breaths per minute," and a minute is 60 seconds, we just multiply: breaths-per-minute = frequency in Hz × 60. So 0.5 Hz = 30 breaths/min.

3Sampling: turning a wiggle into numbers

A phone can't record a smooth continuous curve — it takes a reading, then another, then another, many times a second. Each reading is a sample. How often it reads is the sampling rate, also in hertz. A 50 Hz sampling rate means 50 readings per second.

Analogy. It's like a strobe light flashing on a spinning wheel, or the frames of a movie. Reality is continuous, but you only get snapshots. Enough snapshots and your eye fills in smooth motion. Too few, and things look wrong — think of how wagon wheels in old films seem to spin backwards. That illusion has a name in signal processing: aliasing.

The rule of thumb: to capture a wave faithfully, you need to sample noticeably faster than the wave itself wiggles — at the very least twice per cycle, and comfortably more in practice. Breathing is slow (under 1 Hz), and phones sample motion dozens of times a second, so we have samples to spare. Drag the sampling rate below to see what happens when you don't.

The phone's samples don't arrive perfectly evenly spaced, so the first thing our pipeline does is resample: use straight-line interpolation to lay the data onto a tidy, evenly-spaced grid. That just makes every later step simpler and more reliable.

4Why we filter, and "detrending"

The breathing wobble never shows up alone. Riding along with it are things we don't want:

  • A slow drift — the phone settling, a slow lean, temperature effects. This is an even lower frequency than breathing.
  • Fast noise — jitter, tiny bumps, electrical hiss. Much higher frequency than breathing.

Filtering means keeping the frequencies we care about and suppressing the rest. We know breathing lives in a specific band — roughly 0.1 Hz to 1.0 Hz (that's 6 to 60 breaths per minute). Anything far below or above that band is almost certainly not breathing.

Analogy. It's the bass and treble knobs on a stereo. "Filtering" is just turning down the frequencies you don't want and leaving up the ones you do. We turn down the very-slow drift and the very-fast jitter, and keep the breathing-speed band in the middle.

The simplest, most important filter we apply is detrending: we fit a straight line through the data and subtract it. That line captures the overall offset and slow drift, so removing it re-centers the wobble around zero and flattens out the slow lean.

5Signals are made of simpler waves

Here's the single most important idea on this page: any wiggly signal, no matter how messy, can be built by adding up plain sine waves of different frequencies. A tall slow one, a small fast one, a medium one — stack enough together and you can recreate any shape.

Analogy. A musical chord. Press three piano keys and you hear one combined sound — but it's really three pure notes (three frequencies) added together in the air. Your ear can pick them apart. What we're about to do is give the computer that same ability.

6The Fourier transform & the frequency spectrum

If every signal is a sum of sine waves, the natural question is: given a messy signal, which sine waves is it made of, and how strong is each one? The tool that answers this is the Fourier transform. Think of it as a machine: you feed in a signal measured over time, and it hands back a list of how much of each frequency is present.

Analogy. A glass prism. White light goes in; a rainbow comes out, because the prism splits the light into its component colors. The Fourier transform is a prism for signals: a messy wiggle goes in, and out comes its "rainbow" — the breakdown of which rhythms (frequencies) it contains, and how strong each is.

That rainbow has a name: the frequency spectrum. We plot it with frequency running left (slow) to right (fast), and height showing how much of that frequency is in the signal. A pure sine wave shows up as a single tall spike. Our breathing signal shows up as a spike parked right at the breathing frequency — and finding that spike is how the app gets the bpm.

The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) is just a clever, fast recipe for computing this. It's the exact same idea; "fast" only refers to doing it efficiently. The app uses an FFT because phones are not supercomputers and we want an answer every few seconds.

7The window: being gentle at the edges

The FFT quietly assumes the chunk of signal you give it repeats forever, end joined back to start. But a 30-second slice almost never starts and ends at the same value, so there's a sudden "jump" where the ends meet. The FFT sees that artificial jump as a burst of fake high frequencies, which smears the spectrum and can blur our peak. This smearing is called spectral leakage.

Analogy. A song that cuts off abruptly mid-note — jarring, a click. A song that fades in and fades out is smooth. A window does the fade: we multiply the signal by a gentle curve that's zero at both ends and full in the middle, so the edges taper away instead of slamming shut.

We use a standard, well-behaved fade curve called the Hann window. Below, the raw slice (with its abrupt ends) versus the same slice after windowing. The windowed version eases to zero at both ends, giving a cleaner, sharper spectrum.

8Finding the peak precisely

The spectrum isn't perfectly smooth — it's a row of discrete bars, called bins, each covering a small frequency range. The true breathing frequency usually falls between two bins, so just grabbing the tallest bar rounds us off a little.

To do better, we look at the tallest bar and its two immediate neighbors and fit a little parabola (a smooth U/∩ curve) through those three points. The exact top of that curve lands between the bars and gives a more precise frequency. This is parabolic peak interpolation — a small step that sharpens the final bpm.

Analogy. You have temperature readings at 1:00, 2:00, and 3:00, and you want to know when it was hottest. The peak wasn't necessarily exactly at 2:00 — but by looking at how the three readings curve, you can estimate the true high point fell at, say, 2:12. Same trick, applied to the spectrum.

9Autocorrelation: a second opinion

The spectrum gives us an answer — but a good monitor double-checks itself with a completely different method, and only trusts the answer when both agree. That second method is autocorrelation.

The idea: take the signal, make a copy, and slide the copy sideways in time. At each shift (called a lag) we measure how well the copy lines up with the original. When the shift equals one breathing period, the peaks land on peaks and troughs on troughs — a great match. So the lag where the match score is highest is the breathing period, which we flip into a frequency and a bpm.

Analogy. Clapping along to a song. You try a rhythm, and when your claps line up with the beat, it "locks in" and feels right. Autocorrelation is the signal trying to clap along with a delayed copy of itself; it locks in when the delay matches its own rhythm.

10Confidence: when to trust the number

A number is only useful if we know whether to believe it. The monitor never just shows a bpm blindly — it rates its own confidence from three honest checks:

Prominence. How much taller is the breathing peak than the typical clutter around it in the band? A peak that towers over the noise is trustworthy; one barely poking above the grass is not.
Cycles seen. How many whole breaths actually happened in the window? Measuring a rhythm from 8 breaths is far safer than guessing it from one and a half.
Agreement. Do the spectrum and autocorrelation — two independent methods — land on nearly the same bpm? If they disagree, something's off and we don't pretend otherwise.

From these we assign a tier: high medium low. This is the safety heart of the app. Only a medium-or-better reading is allowed to turn the screen green ("in range") or red (alarm). A low-confidence reading — he shifted the phone, a truck went by, or the breathing signal faded — shows a calm amber "Signal uncertain" instead, and never fires the red alarm. That way a garbage reading can't masquerade as a real too-fast or too-slow result.

Important, and worth repeating. Because a stopped or very weak breath reads as low-confidence, it shows as "Signal uncertain," not a red alarm. This is deliberate and honest — we won't blare a confident-looking alarm off noise — but it means this is not an apnea monitor and not a medical device. A quiet screen never means a child is safe. Never rely on it to watch a sleeping child; if you're worried, look, count manually, and call a clinician.

11The whole pipeline, end to end

Now every piece is on the table. Here's the complete journey, the same one that runs every ~10 seconds on the last 30 seconds of motion data:

1 · Sense. Read gravity's pull on the phone's three axes; breathing tilts it, nudging those readings.
2 · Resample. Lay the uneven samples onto an even time grid (straight-line interpolation).
3 · Detrend. Subtract the best-fit straight line to kill offset and slow drift.
4 · Window. Apply the Hann fade so the edges don't create fake frequencies.
5 · FFT → spectrum. Transform to the frequency world and look only inside the 0.1–1.0 Hz breathing band.
6 · Peak + refine. Take the tallest bar in the band and parabola-interpolate for a precise frequency → ×60 = bpm.
7 · Cross-check. Run autocorrelation independently; require the two to agree.
8 · Confidence → display. Grade it high/medium/low; only confident readings drive the green/red states.

We run that for each of the three axes and keep whichever gives the clearest peak, since the best axis depends on how the phone happens to be lying. Play with the real thing below — set a breathing rate, add noise, and watch the signal pass through every stage to a final bpm and confidence grade.

That's the whole thing. The monitor is doing honest, understandable arithmetic on a faint wobble — clever in the details, but no magic. The most important part isn't the math; it's knowing its limits.
